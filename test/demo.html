1. The Isolation Effect

In cognitive psychology, the von Restorff effect—also recognized as the isolation effect—predicts that when multiple homogeneous stimuli are presented, the stimulus that differs from the rest is significantly more likely to be remembered. When reading dense literature, highlighting creates visual anchors that trigger this effect.

However, unstructured highlighting can lead to the illusion of competence, where learners mistake the physical act of marking a page for actual conceptual mastery. To mitigate this trap, researchers advocate for semantic color categorization.

2. Semantic Color Categorization

By assigning specific cognitive roles to distinct color wavelengths, the brain creates a secondary hierarchical index of the reading material:

  • Yellow: Foundational concepts, central theses, and core arguments.
  • Green: Empirical evidence, statistical proof, and factual case studies.
  • Blue: Domain-specific definitions, terminology, and key jargon.
  • Pink: Contradictions, critical questions, and points requiring scrutiny.
  • Purple: Memorable aphorisms, author quotes, and stylized remarks.
"Color is not merely decorative; in synthesis and active retrieval, it is a low-latency metadata tag for your working memory."

3. From Highlighting to Synthesis

Extracting highlights out of their native context into a unified document or Google Doc transforms passive consumption into active synthesis. When highlights are grouped by category rather than raw chronological sequence, patterns, inconsistencies, and macro-themes immediately emerge.
